What Does an Insurance Agent Do in Malaysia?

By CASB Advisory Team · July 22, 2026 · 7 min read
An insurance agent's work begins before a recommendation is made and continues long after a policy is issued.

Ask someone what an insurance agent does and the most common answer will probably be: sells insurance.

That is part of the role, but it leaves out most of the work. A professional adviser needs to understand a client's circumstances, explain financial risks clearly, manage applications accurately and remain available when service or claims are needed.

For anyone considering this career in Malaysia, here is a realistic look at the responsibilities behind it.

The Short Answer

An insurance agent helps clients identify protection needs and understand suitable insurance solutions. The agent also handles applications, ongoing policy service and claims support while following the insurer's training, documentation and compliance requirements.

The role combines client service, financial education, relationship management, administration and business development.

1. Finding and Speaking With Potential Clients

New advisers need to create opportunities to meet people. This may happen through personal introductions, referrals, professional networking, community activities, educational content or organised agency campaigns.

The purpose of an initial conversation should not be to pressure someone into buying. It is to understand whether the person has a genuine concern that the adviser can help address.

This part of the work requires initiative. A flexible schedule is useful, but meetings do not appear automatically.

2. Understanding the Client Before Recommending Anything

A responsible adviser first gathers relevant information. Depending on the discussion, this may include the client's family responsibilities, income, commitments, existing coverage, health concerns, budget and financial priorities.

Two people of the same age may need very different protection. A parent supporting young children has different concerns from a single professional caring for elderly parents.

Good discovery is less about speaking and more about listening carefully, asking useful questions and avoiding assumptions.

3. Explaining Risks and Possible Solutions

Insurance can involve unfamiliar terms, conditions, exclusions and trade-offs. The adviser's job is to make these understandable without hiding limitations or creating unrealistic expectations.

A recommendation may involve medical protection, life insurance, critical illness coverage, income protection, accident benefits or longer-term planning, depending on the client's needs and eligibility.

The client should understand what a proposed plan is intended to do, what it does not do, what it costs and why it may or may not fit the stated priorities.

4. Preparing Applications and Following Up

Once a client decides to apply, the adviser helps ensure that the application and supporting information are complete and accurate.

This can include identity documents, declarations, payment arrangements and additional information requested during assessment. The adviser may need to coordinate follow-ups while keeping the client informed.

Attention to detail matters. Missing or inaccurate information can create delays and may cause problems later.

5. Servicing Policies After They Begin

The relationship should not end when a policy is issued. Clients may later need help with:

  • Understanding policy information and notices
  • Updating contact or payment details
  • Reviewing protection after marriage, parenthood or a career change
  • Checking nomination or beneficiary information
  • Understanding available service procedures
  • Organising documents when a claim arises

Consistent after-sales service is one of the clearest differences between a short-term salesperson and a long-term adviser.

6. Helping During Claims

Claims are often where clients value their adviser most. A client may be ill, in hospital or dealing with a family emergency, so clear guidance can reduce confusion during a stressful period.

An adviser can explain the general process, help identify required forms and supporting documents, assist with submission and follow up on the claim's progress. The insurer determines the claim outcome according to the policy terms and available evidence; an agent cannot guarantee approval.

7. Training, Compliance and Professional Development

Insurance agents need ongoing learning. Products change, client expectations evolve and advisers must maintain professional standards.

Training may cover product knowledge, fact-finding, communication, digital tools, documentation, ethics, compliance, servicing and claims procedures. New agents also spend time practising conversations and learning from experienced mentors.

The strongest advisers continue learning even after they become experienced.

What Can a Typical Day Look Like?

There is no single timetable, but a working day could include:

  • Morning training or planning with the agency team
  • Following up on client enquiries and service requests
  • Preparing for protection-review appointments
  • Meeting a new prospect in person or online
  • Completing application or claims documentation
  • Checking existing cases and updating clients
  • Creating educational content or requesting referrals
  • Meeting a client outside standard office hours

Experienced advisers may spend more time on reviews, referrals, complex cases and mentoring. New advisers usually devote more time to learning, practising and creating new conversations.

Common Misconceptions

"You must be very extroverted."
Not necessarily. Listening, empathy, preparation and follow-up can matter more than being naturally outgoing.

"Flexible hours mean working whenever you feel like it."
Flexibility gives you control, but clients, deadlines and service responsibilities still require discipline.

"The work finishes after a sale."
A professional adviser's responsibility includes years of service, reviews and claims support.

"Agents decide whether claims are approved."
They do not. Claims are assessed by the insurer according to the policy terms and supporting information.

Is This Type of Work Right for You?

You may enjoy the work if you like meeting people, explaining ideas, solving practical problems and building relationships over time. You should also be willing to handle administration, continue learning and follow up even when there is no immediate reward.

Final Thoughts

An insurance agent does much more than present policies. The real work is understanding people, communicating responsibly, maintaining accurate service and being present when clients need help.

It can be a flexible and meaningful profession, but flexibility comes with accountability. Anyone considering the career should understand both sides before deciding.
